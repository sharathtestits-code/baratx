# Selling BarathX — the Kunal Shah playbook, adapted for where you actually are

This isn't generic "sales tips." It's Kunal Shah's actual operating framework — the one he's talked about publicly for years and used to build CRED — mapped onto BarathX specifically: what to steal, what to adapt, and what to skip because you're pre-scale and he was not (by the time most of this played out).

---

## 1. The core test: does BarathX pass Delta 4?

Shah's central framework: people only permanently change behavior — and become evangelists, not just users — when a product is roughly **4x better** than the old way, not 1.2x better. Below that threshold, the old habit wins by default. Above it, three things happen: people become unable to go back, they tolerate rough edges while you fix them, and — critically for sales — **the product becomes brag-worthy on its own.**

Run this test on BarathX honestly before you sell it to anyone:

- Old way: arguing in an Instagram comment thread that an algorithm buries in an hour, or a WhatsApp group where the "debate" is fifteen people talking past each other.
- BarathX way: a live, sided debate — Agree vs Disagree — where a real person has to answer you, on the record, no algorithm deciding who won.

That gap — *disappearing vs. permanent, algorithmic vs. live, passive vs. actually-in-it* — is your Delta 4 story. Say it in exactly those terms when you sell: not "we have a debate feature," but "the thing you hate about arguing online — that it evaporates and nobody actually answers you — is gone." If a prospect doesn't feel that gap when you explain it, you haven't found your Delta 4 audience yet — don't push the pitch harder, find a different person to pitch.

**Sales application:** open every pitch (user, investor, partner) with the *old way is broken* framing before you describe the product. Problem first, always — this is also literally how your Social brief's post formula works, so your sales pitch and your content should sound like the same person.

## 2. Exclusivity is a marketing tool, not a gate

CRED didn't require a 750+ credit score to keep people out — it required it to make getting in *mean something*. Rejection became the marketing. Acceptance became a status signal people posted about voluntarily.

You already have the raw material for this and are underselling it: **the Founding 100.** Right now it reads like a small referral bonus. Reframe it as membership, not a coupon:

- Don't say: "first 100 people get ₹150."
- Say: "we're only taking 100 Founding members before this closes — you get in by actually posting a real debate that gets real engagement, not by signing up." (This is *already true* per your product — you're just not selling it as exclusivity.)

The mechanism that makes this work isn't the money, it's that **entry is earned by doing the real behavior** (a real debate, real engagement) — same as CRED's credit score, it's a filter that becomes the flex. Someone who got into the Founding 100 has a reason to tell people, because getting in proves something about them, not just about you.

**Sales application:** when you pitch a campus ambassador or an early user, don't frame it as "come join," frame it as "there are only X spots left and you get one by actually doing something, not by signing up" — scarcity that's tied to a real, honest number (per your existing honesty rule — never inflate it).

## 3. Entertain first, explain later — sell the moment, not the feature

CRED almost never explained bill payments in its ads. Indiranagar Ka Gunda, the Venkaboys — pure entertainment, zero feature-listing, and it worked because people shared it for the joke, not the value prop. The product explanation rode in on the back of something people wanted to watch anyway.

You have a version of this sitting in the product already: **real Live debate rooms.** A 20-second clip of an actual heated, funny, or absurd take being argued live is your Indiranagar Ka Gunda — except it's real footage, not a produced ad, which is *more* on-brand for you (per the Social brief: real beats polished, always).

**Sales application:** when pitching meme accounts, campus reps, or even investors, lead with a real clip or a real exchange, not a feature list. "Watch this 20 seconds, then I'll tell you what it is" outperforms "let me explain the product" every time — this is also literally Shah's stated ad philosophy: entertain, then explain.

## 4. Desire over utility — sell status, not features

Shah's line: *"margins are earned from desire, not utility"* — you don't get pricing power or genuine pull by being useful, you get it by making people want to be associated with the thing. His five-star hotel analogy: nobody's paying for a bed, they're paying for what staying there says.

Translate this for BarathX's actual audience (Gen Z, early-career, opinionated): the desire isn't "an app to argue on." It's **"I'm someone who actually thinks, not someone who just reposts."** That's the identity BarathX is selling — the same way CRED sold "I'm the kind of person who pays bills on time and has a great credit score" as an identity, not a utility.

**Sales application:** every pitch — to a user, a campus rep, an investor — should have one line that sells identity, not function. Not "an app where you can debate," but closer to: *"the place where the people who actually have an opinion go, instead of just liking a post and moving on."* Say this like it's already true, because for the people already in it, it is.

## 5. Trust is the actual product — and it's harder for you than it was for CRED

Shah is explicit about this: *"it is obviously harder to trust a start-up than companies that have lasted 50–100 years"* — and CRED spent years building trust before asking for financial-grade behavior. You have it harder in one way (you're pre-scale, no funding headlines to borrow credibility from) and easier in another (you're not asking anyone for money or financial data — the trust bar for "come argue in an app" is much lower than "give us your credit card").

Don't try to borrow trust you don't have yet (fake traction, vague "growing fast" language — this is already correctly banned in your Sales brief's honesty rule). Instead, **sell the honesty itself** as the differentiator, because for your specific audience (Gen Z, allergic to AI-slop and corporate spin per the Social brief's own research) admitting you're small and real is *more* trustworthy than most funded competitors sound right now.

**Sales application:** never hide "we're early." Say it first, on purpose, as a credibility move, not a disclaimer: *"we're small right now, on purpose — the debates happening are genuinely argued, not performed for growth numbers."* This is already how your Sales brief's investor one-liner is written — keep doing exactly that, it's the correct move, not a compromise.

## 6. Referral and rewards should reward the *behavior you want repeated*, not just the signup

CRED didn't just pay for referrals — CRED Coins rewarded on-time payment (the exact behavior CRED needed to become a habit), not just app opens. The lesson isn't "add a rewards program," it's **reward the specific action that makes the product work**, not vanity signups.

For BarathX, the behavior that actually matters is: opening a real debate, or arguing back with a real reply — not installs. Your Founding ₹150 already does this correctly (tied to real engagement, not signup) — the fix is purely in how it's talked about (see #2), not in the mechanic itself.

**Sales application:** if you ever add a referral incentive, tie the reward to *the referred person opening or winning a real debate*, not to them downloading the app. Installs you can buy. Real arguments you can't fake, and that's the whole point of the product.

## 7. What NOT to copy from CRED

Be honest with yourself about the differences, or this playbook backfires:

- **Don't copy the ad spend.** CRED's IPL campaigns ran on venture capital most startups never see. Your version of "entertain first" is a real screen-recorded debate clip shared organically, not a produced celebrity ad — and that's genuinely fine, arguably more credible for your specific audience.
- **Don't copy the exclusivity mechanic literally.** A 750+ credit score is a hard financial filter CRED could verify instantly. Your filter (posting a real debate with real engagement) is behavioral, not verifiable at signup — which is actually better for you: it means the exclusivity is earned through use, not gatekept before anyone tries the product.
- **Don't oversell "engagement business for premium users."** CRED explicitly built for a narrow, high-income cohort. Your Gen Z audience is the opposite of that positioning — don't borrow "premium/luxury" language, borrow the *mechanism* (identity + status, not "expensive/premium").

## 8. The 30-day version of this, concretely

1. Rewrite the Founding 100 pitch everywhere (bio, outreach opener, any copy) from "first 100 get ₹150" to "100 Founding spots, earned by a real debate that gets real engagement — not by signing up." One sentence, no hype.
2. Record and save 3-5 real Live debate clips (10-25 seconds, the funniest or most heated real moments) — these become both your Social Lead's raw material and your Sales Lead's opening line in cold outreach ("watch this, then I'll tell you what it is").
3. In every outreach message (already governed by your Sales brief), add one identity-line before the feature-line — test "the people who actually have an opinion" framing against your current opener and see which gets replies.
4. Never patch over "we're early" — say it first, on purpose, in the same breath as your ask. It's a trust move, not a weakness, for this specific audience.
5. Do not add a rewards mechanic that pays for installs. If you add anything, it pays for a real debate opened or a real reply posted.
